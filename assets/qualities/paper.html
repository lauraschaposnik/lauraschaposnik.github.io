What do good mathematicians say makes them good?

An evidence-based inventory of qualities, drawn from 1015 published interviews, profiles and biographies of 949 people, 884 of them mathematicians with evidence (Phase 1 of a cross-discipline project)

Abstract. We collected 1015 published texts about 949 people (884 mathematicians with evidence), born from the fifth century to the 2000s (most after 1800), and extracted every quality, habit, attitude or circumstance the texts present as part of being a good mathematician. Two language models (Opus and Sonnet) extracted quotes independently; every quote was verified by script as an exact substring of the source, and a third model checked a stratified sample for support. We mapped 47,236 quotes onto a codebook of 183 traits in 12 types and rated each trait for how observable and trainable it is in teenagers and how its importance may change with AI. The most-mentioned qualities are social and motivational, not only intellectual: collaboration (44% of people), love of mathematics (42%), discussing mathematics with peers (41%), mentors and role models (38%), and humility (37%). The strongest pattern in the data is not about era or prizes but about who is speaking: first-person sources (interviews, memoirs) and third-person sources (biographies, obituaries) emphasise different qualities (rank correlation across traits 0.58; only 8 of the top 20 traits are shared). Trait-level coding is moderately reliable (73% agreement, kappa 0.73), so we treat type-level and robust trait-level findings as the substantive results and everything else as hypotheses.

1. Why this study

A high-school programme that wants to develop mathematicians, with and alongside AI tools, needs a defensible answer to the question: which personal qualities do good mathematicians themselves, and those who write about them, say matter? Existing lists tend to come from opinion or from a handful of famous essays. We aimed for a list that is traceable (every claim linked to a short quote and a source), auditable (every automated step has an independent check) and honest about limits. This is Phase 1 (mathematics only). The data schema is discipline-agnostic so other sciences can be added later. A concurrent independent study (Mishra, Beyond Brilliance, EquiTech Futures draft, October 2026) used a similar design; we deliberately built our corpus without using its registry so the two can later be compared.

2. Data

Corpus. 1015 documents: biography 637, interview 145, profile 65, Q&A 63, obituary 42, memoir 22, essay 17, podcast transcript 17, lecture 7. We group them as first-person (the mathematician speaks: interview, Q&A, podcast, memoir, lecture, essay; 271 documents) and third-person (others write about them: biography, obituary, profile; 744 documents). Texts came from open sources (journals, magazines, MacTutor, Celebratio, newsletters, oral-history collections); paywalled and video-only material is absent. The source texts are kept privately for analysis because of copyright; this paper and the website publish only quotes under 25 words with links to the originals.

People. 884 people have at least one audited, mapped quote about them (plus 65 people from neighbouring fields, such as computer science and physics, or with no mapped quote, who are kept but not counted). As reported in their sources: 242 women, 498 men, 144 not stated (we never infer gender). Prize status as reported: 106 with a major prize, 609 none mentioned, 169 unclear. Note that "none mentioned" does not mean no prize was won. Career stage is mostly not stated or posthumous (208 and 513), so we do not analyse by career stage.

3. Methods

  1. Extraction (two independent passes). Pass A used Opus and pass B used Sonnet, each with the same written instructions, reading bundles of about 22,000 words. For every quality presented as part of being a good mathematician, the model recorded a short trait phrase, an exact quote (<25 words), who was speaking, whether the statement is self-described, attributed by others or author framing, and its polarity (a positive quality or a warning against something). Using different model families reduces correlated errors.
  2. Verification. A script confirmed that every quote is an exact substring of the saved text (whitespace and quote-style differences only); rows failing hard rules were rejected (36 rows in total). A third model (Fable) judged 5,378 rows (all risky rows plus a random sample). In the random stratum, 90.8% of quotes were fully supported by their context, 8.7% partly supported and 0.5% not supported; speaker labels were wrong in 2.8% and attribution labels in 4.3%. Partly-supported rows are kept but excluded from "firm" counts.
  3. Codebook. Six independent Opus coders proposed trait names from 300-row samples; a merger consolidated them with the pilot traits into 182 traits (87 attributes, 80 practices, 10 circumstances, 5 beliefs). Codebook v2 added one trait, "Engaging with AI", which recurs in sources published since October 2024; other candidates (initiative, career management, religious faith) did not recur in that window and were left out.
  4. Mapping. Sonnet mapped every row to one trait (or flagged it as not a quality). An independent Opus re-map of 2,250 random rows agreed on the same trait 73.2% of the time (Cohen's kappa 0.73); agreement was 93% when the original mapper was confident, 68% when medium and 38% when low. Disagreements were between neighbouring traits. Consequence: single-trait numbers are soft; the 12-type grouping, defined and approved before this analysis, is more stable.
  5. Types. The 12 types were proposed from the data and approved by the project lead before the results below were computed.
  6. EquiTech ratings. Two raters (Opus, Sonnet) independently rated each trait for observable in teens (high/medium/low), trainable and AI relevance (more important/unchanged/less important), each with a one-line justification. Agreement was 78–83% (kappa 0.64–0.71); on disagreement we kept the lower value, or "unchanged" for AI relevance. These are model judgments, not findings from the sources, and no teacher or mathematician has yet reviewed them.
  7. Statistics. The unit is the person. A trait's prevalence is the share of people with at least one audited quote for it (so one chatty interview cannot dominate), with Wilson 95% intervals. Prevalence measures how often sources mention a quality, not how important it is or whether it causes success. Group contrasts use Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery correction and are labelled exploratory. Everything is computed by script from the database (scripts/paper_stats.py).

4. Results

4.1 The most-mentioned qualities

Social and motivational traits lead. Collaboration, discussing mathematics with peers, mentors and role models, and mentoring students each appear in about a third of people or more, alongside love of mathematics and humility. Classical intellectual traits (commitment to rigour, curiosity, originality, breadth) follow closely. The top of the list is stable under analysis choices: restricting to firm evidence leaves 19 of the top 20 unchanged (rank correlation 0.99); restricting to self-described statements keeps 11 of 20 (rank correlation 0.75).

4.2 Types

Because each person has many quotes, most people have at least one trait in nearly every type: for eleven of the twelve types the share lies between 49% and 94%, so "share of people with any trait of the type" separates types poorly. Only Views on mathematics and mathematicians (23%) is clearly less common, and Rigour and verification is the lowest of the rest (49%). We therefore use types mainly as a navigation aid and report trait-level results with their uncertainty. The full type table is on the website and in the workbook.

4.3 Who is speaking matters more than anything else we tested

Across all traits, prevalence in first-person and third-person sources correlates only 0.58, and just 8 of the top 20 traits are shared. Mathematicians describing themselves emphasise curiosity, love of the subject, thrill of discovery, following their own interests and persistence. Biographers and obituarists emphasise kindness and generosity, originality, inspiring others, landmark results and prolific output, that is, visible outcomes and character as seen from outside. For a training programme this matters: the inner experience of doing mathematics (what students can feel and cultivate) and the outer profile of a successful mathematician are described differently, and both are legitimate evidence.

4.4 Change over time

Confound. Era and genre are almost perfectly entangled. Before 1920, 101 of 104 people appear in third-person sources; from 1970 on, 136 of 339 appear in first-person sources. Apparent trends (for example the lower share of Talent, expertise and output and Teaching and communication in recent decades) may reflect the switch from biographies to interviews, not changing views.

To separate them we compared early (before 1920, n=101) with recent (1970 onward, n=210) people within third-person sources only. Of 163 traits tested, 12 differ at a 5% false-discovery rate with this small sample; the largest shifts are shown below. We regard these as hypotheses, not findings.

4.5 Prize-winners versus others (exploratory)

Comparing the 106 people with a major prize reported against the 609 with none mentioned, 84 of 181 testable traits differ at a 5% false-discovery rate. Prize-winners' sources more often mention seeking simplicity and essence, mathematical intuition, producing landmark results, finding the right framing and choosing problems well; the no-prize group more often mentions applications and interdisciplinary work.

4.6 Which qualities can a programme work with?

Of 183 traits, 68 were rated highly observable in teenagers and 69 highly trainable; 44 were rated high on both. The most-mentioned among them include Mentors and role models, Collaboration, Discussing mathematics with peers, Clear mathematical writing, Hard work, Commitment to rigour, Seeking simplicity and essence, Drive for deep understanding, Self-confidence, Technical mastery. Of all traits, 72 were rated more important in an AI era, 103 unchanged and 8 less important. Common traits that the raters judged both hard to see and hard to train in teenagers, so mainly useful for selection or long-term context rather than teaching, include Producing landmark results, Professional service, Continuing research despite obstacles, Lifelong activity, Improving mathematics education. The interactive EquiTech view lists all traits in a 3×3 grid of observable by trainable with the AI marker.

6. Limitations

  • Genre confound with era (Section 4.4); the older half of the corpus is mostly third-person biography.
  • Estimated dates. Most reference decades are birth year + 40, not measured; 10 people born around 1990–2000 were capped at the decade of their latest source (a proposed rule). 79 people are undated.
  • Sampling. The corpus is a convenience sample of what is accessible online in English: North American and European voices dominate, there are few non-Western historical voices, and prominent mathematicians are over-represented. It is not a random sample of mathematicians, so prevalence figures describe the corpus.
  • Mentions are not importance. Sources discuss what interviewers ask about; prevalence reflects the questions, the genre and what is socially acceptable to say (for example, humility may be easier to attribute to others than to claim for oneself, a hypothesis we have not tested).
  • Model-based coding. All extraction, mapping and rating was done by language models with automated and model audits. Shared blind spots are possible and trait-level mapping is only 73% reproducible. A human-coded validation sample is the most valuable next step.
  • Ratings are judgments about teenagers and AI, not evidence from the sources; they have not been reviewed by teachers.
  • No causal claims. Nothing here shows that a quality makes someone a better mathematician.
  • Mathematics only in this phase; no cross-discipline claims.

7. Implications and next steps

We suggest treating the following as design hypotheses for the programme, to be tested: (1) give collaboration, peer discussion and mentorship the same weight as problem-solving practice, since sources mention them as often as any intellectual trait; (2) build in experiences of curiosity, the thrill of discovery and persistence, which mathematicians themselves emphasise; (3) because AI relevance ratings put judgment, verification and choosing questions at the human end, include explicit practice in these; (4) do not use outcome markers such as prizes or prolific output as targets for teenagers. Next steps: a human-coded validation sample; teacher and mathematician review of the EquiTech ratings; more pre-1950 first-person sources; comparison with the independent Beyond Brilliance data set; and, only after explicit approval, a second discipline.

8. Reproducibility and data availability

Code, prompts (versioned), the audited quote database (short quotes with source links), the codebook, taxonomy, ratings and every audit result are in the private project repository (lauraschaposnik/mathematician-qualities) and can be shared on request. The interactive website (web/index.html) and workbook (outputs/qualities.xlsx) are generated from the same database. Source texts are not republished.
